# Carbohydrate Polymers (Elsevier): what the journal requires

Compiled on 10 Nov 2026 from the journal's own pages: the Guide for authors (sciencedirect.com/journal/carbohydrate-polymers, under Publish), the guideline it links to, *Characterization of carbohydrates and related products* (a five-page PDF dated 26 August 2024), the journal's Insights and Editorial board pages, and the Elsevier pages the guide points to for graphical abstracts and generative AI. The rules below are restated in plain words, with the journal's numbers kept exact; they are not quotations. Items marked *(published articles)* describe how the journal prints papers and are not stated in the guide. Items marked *(Elsevier, general)* come from a publisher-wide page. Guidelines change: when a requirement decides whether the paper is accepted or returned, tell the author to confirm it on the live page.

## 1. Journal facts

- *Carbohydrate Polymers* (abbreviated Carbohydr. Polym.), Elsevier. Its subtitle describes it as a journal for the scientific and technological aspects of industrially relevant polysaccharides, and it places itself in glycoscience. Subject areas listed on the journal page: food science, organic chemistry, biochemistry.
- **The editors are academics.** Three Editors-in-Chief (Tatiana Budtova, Manuel Coimbra and Kevin J. Edgar in Nov 2026) and a team of managing editors, all polysaccharide scientists. This differs from Matter and AFM, where professional in-house editors screen for breadth: here a specialist screens for scope and for how well the polysaccharide is described.
- Submission system: Editorial Manager, reached through "Submit your article" on the journal page. Journal office: carbpol@elsevier.com.
- Review is single anonymized (reviewers know the authors' names), normally by at least two reviewers.
- Timeline shown on the journal page (Nov 2026): 6 days from submission to first decision, 31 days to a decision after review, 89 days to acceptance. Six days to a first decision means a large share of papers is answered by the editor alone.
- A subscription journal with an open-access option. Publishing by subscription costs the author nothing; open access carried a charge of USD 5,230 in Nov 2026.
- Companion open-access journal: *Carbohydrate Polymer Technologies and Applications*, for applications of carbohydrate-containing polymers and oligomers. The editor may offer a transfer through Elsevier's Article Transfer Service.
- After a rejection the journal generally allows one resubmission, and one appeal per submission.

## 2. Scope and article types

### 2.1 The scope rules

These are stated at the top of the guide and decide most outcomes at the editor's desk.

- **A named carbohydrate polymer is the subject.** At least one named carbohydrate polymer must be the main focus of the paper, and it must appear in the title. The polysaccharide's role has to be the major part of the work, not a side topic.
- **The polysaccharide is well characterized.** Structural information that affects its behavior must be given for every polysaccharide and derivative, including ones bought from a supplier, together with how that information was obtained. The guide says editors are unlikely to send a paper for review when the glycan is not adequately characterized. Details in §5.
- **The work is innovative and advances scientific knowledge.**
- **A hypothesis is stated.** The guide says nearly all papers benefit from a statement of hypothesis: concise, declarative, naming the one or more hypotheses the work was meant to confirm or refute. The authors then set it against the most relevant earlier literature (this is how novelty is shown) and say why proving it matters (significance).

Topics the journal lists as of interest: structure–property relationships; analytical methods; chemical, enzymatic and physical modifications; biosynthesis; natural functions; interactions with other materials.

Topics the journal lists as **not** of interest. Check the draft against every line:

| Not of interest | Editing note |
|---|---|
| Bibliometric reviews | |
| Modeling-only studies with no comparison against experimental data (the authors' or published) | |
| Biological, physiological or pharmacological work on non-carbohydrate molecules attached to or mixed with a carbohydrate polymer, unless the polysaccharide has a relevant and specific role | Typical case: a drug-delivery or antibacterial paper where the polysaccharide is only the carrier |
| Materials science of biocomposites where no specific carbohydrate polymer is named, or where its role is not the major part of the study | Typical case: a composite or device paper written around performance |
| Polyalkanoates, polylactic acid, or lignin | A paper whose main subject is one of these is out of scope even if a polysaccharide is present |
| Routine studies of extraction yields without characterization of the polysaccharide obtained under the different conditions | |
| Complexation of a drug with a single cyclodextrin | |
| Newly discovered natural polysaccharides or new derivatives whose structure is unknown | |
| Production and isolation of enzymes that act on polysaccharides | Studies of how an enzyme acts on a polysaccharide are in scope |
| Carbohydrate oligomers with a degree of polymerization of four or less | |
| Treatments of cotton fabric or cellulose-based paper where the work is largely not about the cellulose itself | |
| Carbohydrate polymers used as a support (enzyme immobilization, chromatography) with no specific involvement of the polymer's chemistry | |
| Chars made from polysaccharides, whatever the application | Includes carbonized and activated-carbon materials from cellulose, starch or chitosan |
| Routine preparation of polysaccharides such as cellulose nanocrystals or nanofibers where the focus is the plant source, "new" sources included, and not the science of polysaccharide structure, properties or synthesis | |

### 2.2 Article types and length

**Original full-length research paper** (the default):

- At most **8,000 words of text, excluding references**. The guide does not say whether the abstract, captions and tables count. Count the whole file except the reference list; a paper under 8,000 on that count is safe under any reading.
- Generally no more than **15 figures and tables in total**.
- The same information must not appear in both a figure and a table.
- Not published before, except in preliminary form.

**Other types:**

| Type | Limits |
|---|---|
| Review | At most 18,000 words excluding references, at most 20 figures and tables; normally built on the literature of the previous five years. The journal sets a very high bar: in-depth, covering failures and challenges as well as advances, and of the highest quality on first submission, without need for heavy reworking or language correction. Bibliometric reviews are out of scope. |
| Letter to the Editor | About 800 words excluding references, on a paper published in the journal; write to carbpol@elsevier.com first. |

## 3. What is needed at submission

Unlike Matter and AFM, **this guide offers no free-format first submission**. The format in §4–§7 applies to the first version, and with a first decision in about a week, a paper that ignores it gives the editor an easy reason to return it. Tell the author this plainly: here nothing "can wait until the journal asks" except the items after acceptance.

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Manuscript file | Editable: Word (.doc, .docx) or LaTeX (.tex). A PDF is not accepted as the source file. Word files in a single column. No leftover strikethrough or underlining (accept all tracked changes first). |
| Numbering | **Page numbers and continuous line numbers** throughout. |
| Title page | Title, authors, affiliations, corresponding author with email (§4). |
| Abstract and keywords | Abstract at most 200 words; 3 to 6 keywords. |
| Materials and methods | In the main body, immediately after the Introduction. Not in the supplementary material. |
| Figures and tables | In the body of the article where they are discussed, not gathered at the end. The general part of the guide also asks for each figure as a separate file (Figure_1, Figure_2 ...). Do both. |
| Graphical abstract | **Required at submission**, as a separate file (§7). |
| Supplementary material | Uploaded with the manuscript; each file with a short caption and cited in the text. |
| Declaration of competing interests | Made with Elsevier's declarations tool (declarations.elsevier.com), which produces a Word file to upload. Always required, also when there is nothing to declare. No signatures needed. |
| Funding statement | In the manuscript (§4). |
| Author contributions | CRediT roles for every author (§4). The guide makes this a requirement of the corresponding author. |
| Research data | The journal applies Elsevier's "Option C": deposit the research data in a suitable repository and give the link during submission. If the data cannot be shared, a statement explaining why is entered during submission and is published with the article. |
| AI declaration | If an AI tool helped prepare the text: a statement in the manuscript at first submission (§8). |
| Ethics statements | For work with people or animals (§8). |
| Cover letter | The guide prescribes its content only for a resubmission (§9). |

The upload pages of Editorial Manager may ask for things the guide does not mention, for example suggested reviewers or Highlights. The guide as read on 10 Nov 2026 does not ask for Highlights. Where an Elsevier journal's system does ask, the publisher's standard is three to five bullet points of at most 85 characters each, spaces included, in a separate Word file *(Elsevier, general)*. Draft them as an optional extra so the author is not stopped in the middle of an upload.

**Revision and resubmission.** A revised paper needs a point-by-point response. A paper resubmitted after rejection must say so in the cover letter, with the previous title and manuscript number, answer every comment of the reviewers and editor, and include a "Response to Reviewers" document that reproduces the original reviews with detailed answers, the two visibly distinguished (the guide suggests colors). It should reflect substantial revision, with new experiments where the reviewers' concerns need them.

## 4. Manuscript components, in order

The guide fixes two things about structure: sections are numbered, and Materials and methods follows the Introduction. The order of the remaining parts below is the one published articles use *(published articles)*; the typesetters arrange the end matter, so where the guide places two sections "before the reference list" either order is accepted.

Title page → Abstract → Keywords → 1. Introduction → 2. Materials and methods → 3. Results and discussion → 4. Conclusions → CRediT authorship contribution statement → Declaration of competing interest → Declaration of generative AI and AI-assisted technologies in the manuscript preparation process → Data availability → Funding → Acknowledgements → Appendices and supplementary data → References.

**Title.** Concise and informative. Avoid abbreviations and formulae unless they are established and widely understood. By the scope rule it names the carbohydrate polymer the paper is about: "chitosan", "cellulose nanocrystals", "high-amylose maize starch", not "a biopolymer" or "a polysaccharide". No length limit or capitalization rule is given; the journal prints titles in sentence case *(published articles)*, and a title that runs past two lines is not concise.

**Authors.** Given name(s) and family name(s) of each author, in the same order as in the submission system, spelled with care. An author may add their name in their own script in parentheses.

**Affiliations.** Where the work was done. A lower-case superscript letter after each author's name and in front of the matching address; the full name and postal address of each affiliation, with the country.

**Corresponding author.** One person handles correspondence during review and after publication. Their email address goes on the title page and is published. A present or permanent address, if different, is a footnote marked with a superscript Arabic numeral.

**The author list is final at submission.** The editors generally will not consider changes once the paper is submitted; a change needs a form, a reason, written agreement from every author and the editor's approval, and none is allowed after acceptance. Ask the author to check names and order now.

**Abstract.** At most 200 words (the guide says so twice). It states the purpose, outlines the procedures, gives the main findings with their statistical significance where that applies, and the principal conclusions, with the weight on what is new. It must stand alone. Avoid references; if one is essential, give authors and year. Avoid uncommon abbreviations, or define them at first mention. The abstract is not a numbered section.

**Keywords.** 3 to 6, in English. Avoid keywords made of several words joined by "and" or "of". Abbreviations only when firmly established in the field.

**Sections.** Numbered: 1, then 1.1, 1.1.1, 1.1.2, 1.2 and so on. Headings on their own line. Cross-references use the section number ("see Section 2.3"), not "the text".

**1. Introduction.** States the problem, the most relevant earlier work and the hypothesis (§2.1). The usual place for the hypothesis is the last paragraph, followed by one sentence on how it differs from the earlier work and one on why it matters.

**2. Materials and methods.** Directly after the Introduction, in the main text. It carries the characterization of every polysaccharide (§5). Methods already published are cited by reference, with only the relevant changes described, but the text must still give the minimum a reader needs to understand what was done: reagent, concentration, time, temperature and the kind of derivative, for example. A bare "as reported by (citation)" is not acceptable, because it sends the reader to another paper. Routine chromatographic conditions may be covered by a reference, with the chromatograms in the supplementary material. Mechanical tests need sample dimensions, temperature and humidity. Statistical methods and ethics statements go here.

**3. Results and discussion** (or two separate sections) and **4. Conclusions** *(published articles)*. The Conclusions should answer the hypothesis: confirmed, refuted, or confirmed within stated limits.

**CRediT authorship contribution statement.** Required. One entry per author with their roles from the CRediT list: conceptualization; data curation; formal analysis; funding acquisition; investigation; methodology; project administration; resources; software; supervision; validation; visualization; writing – original draft; writing – review and editing. Printed form *(published articles)*: "First Author: Conceptualization, Investigation, Writing – original draft. Second Author: Supervision, Writing – review and editing."

**Declaration of competing interest.** The statement that the declarations tool produces. Do not write one for the author.

**AI declaration.** A new section placed before the reference list (§8).

**Data availability.** Where the deposited data are (repository and link), or why they cannot be shared.

**Funding.** Funders are listed in a set form: "This work was supported by <funder> [grant number <n>]; <funder> [grant numbers <n>, <n>]." The role of any sponsor in the study design, the data, the writing or the decision to submit is stated, or it is stated that there was none. If there was no funding, the guide gives a fixed sentence saying that the research received no specific grant from public, commercial or not-for-profit funders (the author copies it from the "Funding sources" part of the guide).

**Acknowledgements.** A separate section directly before the reference list, and nowhere else (not on the title page, not as a footnote). It names people who helped, including with language or proofreading.

**Appendices.** Labeled A, B. Equations inside them are numbered Eq. (A.1), tables Table A.1, figures Fig. A.1. Published articles carry a line "Appendix A. Supplementary data" pointing to the supplementary material *(published articles)*.

**Footnotes.** Sparingly; numbered consecutively.

**References.** See §6.

## 5. Characterization of the polysaccharide

From the journal's guideline *Characterization of carbohydrates and related products*. Its opening rule: all materials deriving from new or commercial polysaccharides must be characterized in enough detail for the work to be reproduced. This is the commonest reason a materials paper is returned by this journal, because materials scientists treat a purchased polysaccharide as a reagent and give only the supplier's name.

For every polysaccharide in the draft, find the items below or put them in the to-do list, in the group that decides whether the paper goes to review. Never supply a value; only the author or the supplier's certificate has it.

The polymer-specific rows in §5.1 apply to that kind of polysaccharide however it was obtained. For material made in the laboratory or supplied by a mill or a colleague, the source, the starting material and how it was prepared take the place of supplier and catalogue code; a lab-made derivative also falls under §5.3.

### 5.1 Purchased (commercially available) polysaccharides

Always: supplier and catalogue code; purity (for example the total carbohydrate content and the percentage of each monosaccharide residue); molecular weight (§5.2). And by polymer:

| Polymer | Also required |
|---|---|
| Alginate | At least the fraction of G residues (F_G) or the M/G ratio. The full composition from NMR, for example as a table, if the detailed structure is expected to influence the study. |
| Pectin | Degree of methyl esterification; galacturonic acid content. |
| Chitosan | Degree of deacetylation. |
| Xanthan gum | Degree of pyruvylation and of acetylation. |
| Red seaweed galactans (agar, agarose, carrageenans) | Ratio galactose : 3,6-anhydrogalactose : sulfate. |
| Glycosaminoglycans and other sulfated glycans (heparin, chondroitin sulfate, fucoidan, ulvan) | Degree of sulfation. |
| Heteropolysaccharides in general | Monosaccharide composition. |
| Nanocellulose (cellulose nanocrystals, nano- and microfibers) | How it was prepared. Both lateral and longitudinal dimensions from AFM or TEM; a hydrodynamic radius from DLS is not enough. Surface charge type and density, or the number of charged groups, and the counter-ion; zeta potential alone is not enough. For nano- and microfibrillated cellulose, the composition: cellulose, hemicelluloses, and lignin if any. |
| Nanochitin | How it was prepared; lateral and longitudinal dimensions from AFM or TEM; surface charge density, or the degree of surface deacetylation. |
| Cellulose derivatives (esters, ethers) | Nature of the substituents and their degree of substitution (DS), with the molar substitution (MS) where it applies, as for hydroxyalkyl ethers. The position of substitution is often relevant and useful. |
| Native starch | Botanical source and supplier catalogue number; amylose content; purity (non-starch components); the extraction method if extracted in the laboratory. The molecular weight or size of amylose and amylopectin, with the method, if it affects the results. |
| Modified starch (chemical, enzymatic, physical) | Details of the modification process; degree of modification or substitution; molecular and supramolecular structural details; the methods used. |

### 5.2 Molecular weight

- Give the method in enough detail, and the units (g/mol or Da).
- Name the type of average (Mn, Mw, Mv ...), unless the sample is essentially uniform. Different methods give different averages.
- **A molecular-weight interval is not acceptable** ("100–300 kDa", as printed on many supplier labels).
- With light scattering, alone or combined with GPC/SEC: give the dn/dc value.
- With SEC/GPC calibrated against standards of a different polymer, the values are apparent molecular weights. That is acceptable when it is said clearly in the text, figures and tables, with the columns, the flow rate, the detector and its angle, and the name of the commercial standard.
- Solution viscosities may be reported, with concentration, solvent, temperature and shear rates.
- Molecular weight from intrinsic viscosity: all conditions (temperature, solvent) and the Mark–Houwink–Sakurada constants with their citation. Such values are usually not viscosity averages.
- If the study uses a single molecular weight, the choice must be justified. If molecular weight is believed not to influence the results, that must be argued. The same applies to the degree of substitution. Where a draft uses one grade of a polysaccharide, add a request for that one sentence to the characterization marker.
- **"Polydispersity index" and "PDI" are not to be used.** The term is dispersity, symbol Đ.

### 5.3 Polysaccharides whose structure is already known, and new polymers derived from them

Purity; molecular weight; sugar and linkage composition; degree of substitution and, where possible, where the new groups are.

### 5.4 Polysaccharides whose structure was not known before

- Purity and molecular weight.
- Monosaccharide composition, including the absolute configuration when the source is bacterial or a seaweed. The methods must say whether peak ratios used a calibration or peak areas.
- Linkage analysis, preferably with deuterium labeling at the anomeric position, and at C6 for uronic-acid-containing polysaccharides. The EI-MS spectra of the derivatives go in the supplementary material for the reviewers.
- NMR. The minimum is the assignment of the cross-peaks of the ¹H–¹H COSY, ¹H–¹H TOCSY and ¹H–¹³C HSQC (or HMQC) spectra, to establish the anomeric configuration (α or β) and ring size (furanose or pyranose) of each residue. For a heterogeneous polysaccharide the assignment may be limited to the most intense signals, with the nature and location of non-stoichiometric substituents defined. The sequence of residues (from HMBC, NOESY or ROESY) is not mandatory and counts in the paper's favor. Further spectra that cross-check the deductions are welcome.
- The nature of any non-carbohydrate substituents (acyl, alkyl, sulfate, phosphate and others).
- To judge whether a polysaccharide from a bacterial or plant source is new, authors should consult the Carbohydrate Structure Database (csdb.glycoscience.ru).

### 5.5 Sample history

Polysaccharides such as xanthan and scleroglucan (schizophyllan) change conformation with solvent and temperature, show hysteresis on heating and cooling, and may be mixtures of multi-stranded and single-stranded forms. The sample's history and treatments must be given.

### 5.6 Techniques that do not prove fine structure

Not accepted as evidence of monosaccharide residues, ring size or anomeric configuration, for new and commercial polysaccharides alike:

- **Microscopy (SEM, TEM, AFM).** It is suitable for the features of materials made from the glycan and for nanocellulose dimensions.
- **Routine FT-IR.** Spectra of different polysaccharides differ too little. It is accepted as qualitative evidence that a chemical modification took place. Quantitative use needs a calibration, reported in the paper.
- **Routine UV** on isolated oligo- or polysaccharides, which lack diagnostic chromophores.
- **DLS** for nanocellulose dimensions, and **zeta potential alone** for nanocellulose charge.

Editing consequence: a sentence such as "FT-IR confirmed the structure of the polysaccharide" has to become what the spectrum can support ("FT-IR showed the ester carbonyl band at <value> cm⁻¹, consistent with acetylation"), with a query for the quantitative evidence. If the draft does not say which group a band belongs to, do not assign it; ask. Keep the authors' DLS, zeta-potential and microscopy results in the paper: they are data, only not sufficient for this purpose. Reword what they are said to show and query the measurement the journal accepts.

### 5.7 Reporting conventions

- Absolute configuration: the descriptors D and L in small capitals (or two points smaller than the text). Plain text cannot carry this; name it once in the to-do list.
- Anomeric configuration: α or β, upright (not italic).
- NMR: ¹H chemical shifts with two decimals, ¹³C with one. Chemical shifts as δ or in ppm, one convention throughout, never both. In a chemical-shift table the first column gives the residue's letter with its anomeric configuration, its mode of substitution and the location of any non-carbohydrate substituents.
- Linkage analysis: preferably a table with the methylated and acetylated positions of each alditol (or PMAA) or the deduced linkage; proportions as whole numbers if in percent, at most two decimals if ratios; the column heading says whether they are area- or mole-based; the diagnostic fragments are listed.
- Deduced linkages omit the anomeric position: "2-Glc*p*" or "2-Glc", not "1,2-Glc*p*"; "3-GlcN". The "*p*" for pyranose may be dropped; the furanose form must be shown. Where the ring size is not settled, the table lists both possibilities.
- Molecular modeling or docking studies: coordinates of the molecules shown (PDB or another accessible format) as supplementary data.
- Drawings of polysaccharides: the symbol nomenclature for glycans (SNFG) is recommended for natural polysaccharides; chair drawings are useful for showing stereochemistry. In a repeat-unit drawing the linkage oxygen sits inside the left bracket, the terminal H outside it, and the OH of the terminal anomeric position outside the right bracket.

## 6. Style and references

**Language.** American or British English, not a mixture. Keep the variety the author used and make it consistent; do not convert a British draft to American for this journal. The submission checklist expects spelling and grammar to have been checked. The guide also asks for inclusive language.

**Units and symbols.**

- SI units; any other unit with its SI equivalent.
- Variables in italics. Equations as editable text, never as pictures; simple formulae in the line of text; a solidus (/) for small fractions; powers of e written with exp; displayed equations numbered consecutively in the order they are cited.
- The conventions in §5.7 for carbohydrate names, NMR data and linkages.
- The guide does not prescribe "Fig." or "Figure". Published articles print "Fig. 1" and "Table 1" *(published articles)*; the guide's own appendix format is "Fig. A.1". Use "Fig." in callouts and captions, consistently.
- Panel labels and unit style (slash or negative exponent) are not prescribed. Keep the author's, consistently.

**Citations in the text.** The style of the American Psychological Association, 7th edition: author and year. The guide refers to the APA manual and does not restate its rules, which are: one author (Surname, 2021); two authors (Surname & Surname, 2021); three or more (Surname et al., 2021) from the first citation; in running text "Surname et al. (2021) reported ..."; several works in one parenthesis in alphabetical order separated by semicolons; two works by the same author(s) in the same year distinguished as 2021a, 2021b.

**Reference list.**

- Alphabetical by first author, then chronological. No numbers.
- Every work cited in the text is in the list, and every entry in the list is cited.
- The guide states that every reference must correspond to a real, existing source, and reminds authors that AI-generated references can be wrong or invented.
- DOIs where available.
- Unpublished results and personal communications are better mentioned in the text than listed. "In press" means accepted.
- Web references: the full URL and the date last accessed.
- Data sets: authors, title, repository, version, year, persistent identifier, with the tag [dataset] before the entry.
- Preprints: marked as such, with their DOI; if the work has since been published, cite the published version.
- Reference managers that support CSL have the journal's style. Authors who use one should remove the field codes before submitting.

Patterns (journal names in full, article titles in sentence case):

- Journal article: `Surname, A. B., Surname, C. D., & Surname, E. F. (Year). Title of the article. Full Journal Name, volume, first–last page. https://doi.org/...`
- Article with an article number: `Surname, A. B., & Surname, C. D. (Year). Title of the article. Full Journal Name, volume, Article 123456. https://doi.org/...`
- Book: `Surname, A. B., & Surname, C. D. (Year). Title of the book (2nd ed.). Publisher.`
- Chapter: `Surname, A. B., & Surname, C. D. (Year). Title of the chapter. In E. F. Editor, & G. H. Editor (Eds.), Title of the book (pp. first–last). Publisher.`
- Data set: `Surname, A. B., & Surname, C. D. (Year). Title of the data set [dataset]. Repository, version. https://doi.org/...`

Editing rule: if the draft uses numbered citations and the reference list was supplied, convert each number to author and year from the author's own list, sort the list alphabetically and remove the numbers; say so in the change log. If the list was not supplied, leave the numbers and put one marker at the head of the Introduction. Keep each journal name as the author wrote it and say once that the style uses full journal names. Add a marker to any entry that lacks authors, year, title, volume or pages. Never complete an entry from memory.

## 7. Figures, tables and graphical abstract

**How many.** Generally at most 15 figures and tables together for a research paper. Nothing shown twice, once as a figure and once as a table.

**Figures.**

- Cited in the text, numbered in the order they appear, placed in the body where they are discussed, and also uploaded as separate files with logical names.
- Every figure has a caption: a brief title (not shown on the figure itself) and a description that explains every symbol and abbreviation.
- File types: vector drawings as EPS or PDF with fonts embedded; photographs as TIFF, JPG or PNG at 300 dpi or more (at least 1063 pixels wide for a single column, 2244 for a full page); bitmapped line drawings at 1000 dpi or more; charts may be XLS or XLSX; annotated images PPT or PPTX with legible text.
- Not accepted: low resolution; lettering much too small for the image; several images or graphs combined in one file where they are not meant to be compared with each other.
- Color is free online. Choose colors that readers with impaired color vision can tell apart.

**Tables.** Editable text, never pictures. Next to the text that discusses them, cited, numbered in order of appearance, each with a caption, notes below the table body. No vertical rules, no shaded cells. Use tables sparingly and do not repeat data given elsewhere.

**Image integrity.** No feature of an image may be enhanced, obscured, moved, removed or added. Brightness, contrast and color balance may be adjusted if nothing is hidden. Nonlinear adjustments such as gamma are disclosed in the caption. AI tools must not create or alter images of primary research data (micrographs, gels, spectra images). The journal may screen images and ask for the originals.

**Supplementary material.** Elsevier's term (published articles also say "Supplementary data"). It appears online exactly as received, unedited. It must not hold the Materials and methods. It is the place for EI-MS spectra of linkage derivatives, chromatograms and modeling coordinates (§5).

**Graphical abstract.** Required at submission, as a separate file. One image that summarizes the article in a pictorial form for readers from other disciplines.

- At least 1328 pixels wide, the longest side horizontal, readable at a width of 13 cm.
- *(Elsevier, general)* The minimum size is 1328 × 531 pixels at 300 dpi; larger images keep the same 5:2 proportion. Fonts: Times, Arial, Courier or Symbol, large enough to read when the image is reduced. A clear start and end, reading left to right or top to bottom. No heading "Graphical abstract" inside the image, no separate caption or text outside it, little empty space.
- File types: TIFF, EPS, PDF or MS Office files.
- Third-party material needs permission.
- Use of AI must follow Elsevier's policy, which as read on 10 Nov 2026 does not allow general-purpose generative AI image tools for graphical abstracts.

Do not generate the image. Describe a landscape layout the author can draw.

## 8. Policies that affect the text

**Generative AI in writing** (Elsevier policy). Authors may use AI tools with human oversight and stay accountable for the content, including the accuracy of anything generated and of every reference. Use must be declared when the paper is first submitted, in a new section placed before the reference list, titled *Declaration of generative AI and AI-assisted technologies in the manuscript preparation process*. The template sentence is the Elsevier one that Matter also uses (`deliverable-templates.md` §13): the authors name the tool and the reason, and state that they reviewed and edited the content and take full responsibility for it. Basic grammar, spelling and reference tools are exempt. An assistant that rewrites sentences is more than that, so an author who uses this skill includes the statement. AI tools cannot be authors.

**Generative AI in images.** Allowed for explanatory illustrations (flow charts, schematic diagrams) when disclosed in the caption and in the AI declaration. Not allowed for creating or altering primary research images.

**Originality.** Not published before (a preprint, abstract, lecture or thesis does not count), not under consideration elsewhere, approved by all authors. Manuscripts may be checked with screening tools.

**Preprints.** Allowed. During submission the author can choose to post the manuscript on SSRN, where it becomes public once it passes the desk review; all co-authors must agree first. The choice has no effect on the editorial decision.

**Authorship.** Every author contributed to the conception, data or analysis, to drafting or revising, and approved the submitted version. One corresponding author. Changes after submission are generally refused (§4).

**People and animals.** Work with human participants, their data or their tissue needs a statement of the ethics approval (with its date and reference number), the guidelines followed and informed consent; if approval was not required, the statement explains why. This covers sensory panels and volunteers who wear or test a material. Animal work follows the ARRIVE guidelines, names the rules complied with and the approval number, and states the sex of the animals.

**Resubmission and appeal.** Generally one resubmission after rejection (§3); one appeal per submission, under Elsevier's appeal policy.

**Proofs.** Corrections are expected within two days of receiving the proof.

## 9. Cover letter

The guide says what a cover letter must contain only for a resubmission: that it is one, with the previous title and manuscript number. For a first submission the letter is the place to answer the editor's screening questions in half a page (editorial judgment, built on the scope rules in §2.1):

1. Which carbohydrate polymer the paper is about, and that it is the subject and not a component.
2. The hypothesis, in one sentence, and whether the results confirm it.
3. How it differs from the most relevant earlier work (one or two references).
4. That the polysaccharide is characterized as the journal's guideline asks, naming the key values.
5. The formal statements: original, not under consideration elsewhere, approved by all authors.

Skeleton in `deliverable-templates.md` §12.

## 10. Why papers are declined without review

The guide's own statements: the glycan is not adequately characterized; the topic is on the not-of-interest list; the work is not innovative. In editing terms the risks are:

- **The polysaccharide is an ingredient.** The paper is about a device, a drug, a nanoparticle or a composite's performance, and the polysaccharide could be exchanged for another polymer without changing the story.
- **The title does not name the carbohydrate polymer**, or names it after the application.
- **A purchased polysaccharide is described by its supplier only**: no molecular weight, no purity, no degree of deacetylation, substitution or esterification, no M/G ratio.
- **Molecular weight as a range** copied from the label, or "PDI".
- **Structure claimed from FT-IR, UV or microscopy**; nanocellulose size from DLS and charge from zeta potential only.
- **No hypothesis.** The Introduction ends with "herein, we prepared ..." and never says what was expected and why.
- **Routine work**: extraction yields from another plant, nanocellulose from a "new" source, a drug with one cyclodextrin, a char.
- **The paper's main polymer is lignin, PLA or a polyhydroxyalkanoate.**
- **Formal faults**: more than 8,000 words or 15 figures and tables; an abstract over 200 words; methods in the supplementary material; no line numbers; no graphical abstract; mixed British and American spelling; numbered citations.
- **Language** that needs heavy correction, or prose that reads as machine-written.

## 11. Submission checklist

Decides whether the editor sends the paper to reviewers:

- [ ] A named carbohydrate polymer is the main focus of the paper and appears in the title
- [ ] The topic is not on the journal's not-of-interest list (§2.1)
- [ ] Every polysaccharide, purchased ones included: supplier and catalogue code, purity, molecular weight with method, units and type of average (no interval), and the polymer-specific items in §5.1
- [ ] New or modified polysaccharides: the structural evidence in §5.3 or §5.4; no fine-structure claim resting on FT-IR, UV or microscopy
- [ ] "Dispersity (Đ)" in place of "polydispersity index" or "PDI"
- [ ] A hypothesis stated in the Introduction, set against the most relevant earlier work, and answered in the Conclusions
- [ ] Key numbers with replicates, error and, where differences are claimed, a statistical test

Format and files, all needed for the first submission:

- [ ] Word or LaTeX file, single column, tracked changes accepted; page numbers and continuous line numbers
- [ ] Text at most 8,000 words excluding references; at most about 15 figures and tables; nothing shown as both figure and table
- [ ] Title page: concise title without uncommon abbreviations or formulae; full names; affiliations with superscript letters and full postal addresses; one corresponding author with email
- [ ] Abstract at most 200 words, standing alone, no citations, no undefined abbreviations
- [ ] 3 to 6 keywords, none joined with "and" or "of"
- [ ] Numbered sections; Materials and methods in the main text directly after the Introduction
- [ ] Methods described in enough words to be understood without opening the cited paper
- [ ] Figures and tables in the body where discussed, each with a caption; figures also as separate files; tables editable
- [ ] Citations as author and year (APA 7th); reference list alphabetical, unnumbered, complete, with DOIs; every entry cited and every citation listed
- [ ] One variety of English throughout; SI units
- [ ] CRediT authorship contribution statement
- [ ] Declaration of competing interest from the declarations tool, uploaded as a Word file
- [ ] Funding statement in the standard form, or the no-funding sentence
- [ ] Data deposited in a repository with the link ready, or a statement of why the data cannot be shared
- [ ] AI declaration section before the reference list, if an AI tool helped with the text
- [ ] Ethics and consent statements if people or animals were involved
- [ ] Graphical abstract: separate file, landscape, at least 1328 pixels wide, readable at 13 cm, drawn by the authors
- [ ] Supplementary material uploaded with captions and cited in the text
- [ ] Permissions for any reused figures
- [ ] Optional, if the submission system asks: Highlights (3 to 5, each at most 85 characters with spaces)
- [ ] For a resubmission: cover letter naming the previous title and manuscript number, and a Response to Reviewers document
