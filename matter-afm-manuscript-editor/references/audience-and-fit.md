# Audience and fit: what the editors of each journal are looking for

At all three journals an editor reads every submission before any reviewer does, and decides within days. So the first decision on a paper is about fit and clarity, and the three journals ask different questions.

- **Matter and Advanced Functional Materials** are run by professional in-house editors and accept a first submission in any format. Their question: can an editor who is a materials scientist, but not in the author's subfield, see within two minutes what was found and why it matters to the journal's readers?
- **Carbohydrate Polymers** is run by academic editors who are polysaccharide scientists, and it expects its format from the first submission. Their question: is a named, well-characterized polysaccharide the subject of this paper, and what does the paper add to polysaccharide science?

This file is the guide for those questions. Use it to give the author a fit verdict, to choose between the journals, and to reframe the title, abstract, introduction and closing section.

Quotations in §1 and §2 are from the journals' own pages (read 10 Nov 2026; sources listed in `matter-requirements.md` and `afm-requirements.md`). §3 restates the Carbohydrate Polymers guide in plain words (sources in `carbpol-requirements.md`). Statements about what the journals publish "in practice" are editorial judgment drawn from the journals' scope and published papers, and are labeled as such.

## 1. Matter

**Who reads it.** The whole materials community and its neighbors. "Cell Press journals are read by audiences across multiple disciplines. Therefore, it is essential that all manuscripts be accessible to a broad readership interested in diverse scientific fields." The journal calls itself "the home for multi-disciplinary, transformative materials science research" and describes its community as "physicists, chemists, and engineers".

**The bar.** "Submitted articles must report a new concept or discovery that will be of interest to the general materials community." And the sentence that decides most rejections: "Submissions deemed to be routine, derivative, incremental, or excellent yet not of general interest will not be considered."

Note the last phrase. A paper can be excellent and still be declined because only one subfield would read it. The editing task is to make the general interest visible, and to tell the author plainly when it is not there.

**What the editor asks.** The cover-letter instructions are the editor's questions: what was previously known, what is "the conceptual advance provided by your work", and what is "the significance of the work and its potential impact". If the draft cannot answer the second one in a sentence, the paper is not ready for this journal regardless of formatting.

A conceptual advance is something a reader can take away and use on a different material: a design principle, a mechanism, a previously unseen behavior, a method. "A lignin-reinforced hydrogel with 3.2 MJ m⁻³ toughness" is a result. "Sacrificial hydrogen bonds between lignin and the network dissipate energy without covalent crosslinks, a route that should apply to other polyphenols" is a concept supported by that result.

**What the form of the paper tells you about the audience.**

- The title must "capture the broad conceptual significance of the work" in about 10–12 words.
- The summary must end with "an indication of the broader significance of the work".
- Results subheadings state findings, so a reader can follow the argument from the headings alone.
- The Discussion "should explain the implications of the work and place them into a broader context".
- The Progress and potential box is written for non-experts and asks about longer-term ambitions and impact on society.
- The MAP level tells readers how far the material is from use. Proposing an honest level is part of fitting in; a paper that claims application-readiness on proof-of-concept data reads as overselling.

**What the journal says it dislikes.** "We discourage novelty claims (e.g., use of the word 'novel' and 'first') because they are overused, tend not to add meaning, and are difficult to verify." Significance has to be shown by the comparison with what was known.

**In practice** (judgment). Matter papers tend to have a single clear idea, a mechanism that is tested and not only proposed, and a demonstration that makes the idea concrete. Papers that optimize a composition, or report a better number on a familiar system without explaining why, are the "incremental" case. Papers at MAP 1–4 dominate.

## 2. Advanced Functional Materials

**Who reads it.** "Materials scientists, chemists, physicists, ceramicists, engineers, metallurgists." The guide's first instruction to authors is to "bear in mind the interdisciplinary nature of the readership" and "emphasize the importance of the topic being presented to the research community, extending beyond specialists in the field."

**The bar.** The journal "reports breakthrough research in all aspects of materials science". A Research Article's "essential findings should be novel" and "conclusions must be adequately supported by the data". The ethics policy adds: "The manuscript should represent significant progress in the field under study, as well as being presented in the context of previously published work (by the authors and other researchers) by giving due reference, especially to the latest international research."

**How the first screen works.** "Manuscripts are rejected by the editors at this stage if they are insufficiently original, have serious scientific flaws, need extensive improvement in the language, or do not fall within the aims and scope of the journal." Three of those four are visible in the title, abstract and introduction. Disagreement about novelty or significance is explicitly not a ground for appeal, so the paper gets one chance to make its case.

**Function** (judgment, from the journal's name and what it publishes). An AFM paper shows a material doing something. There is a measured functional property (conduction, sensing, actuation, adhesion, barrier, optical response, energy storage or conversion, self-healing, antibacterial action, separation, thermal management) and usually a demonstration of it in use. A paper that stops at synthesis and structural characterization, or whose only function claim is a sentence of outlook, is likely to be judged outside the scope. Ask of every draft: what is the function, where is it measured, and against what?

**What the form of the paper tells you about the audience.**

- The title "should clearly describe the research using relevant keywords and it should avoid catchy but non-descriptive phrases". Readers find AFM papers by search; the title is for indexing as much as for appeal.
- The abstract is impersonal and in the present tense, 200 words at most. It reads as a statement of what the material is and does.
- The Introduction is expected to summarize "the objectives and main conclusions of the work", so the last paragraph states the result, with numbers.
- The Table of Contents text is "for a general audience" in 50–60 words.
- The Experimental Section must disclose every composition and end with a Statistical Analysis subsection. The journal's detailed characterization rules signal a readership that will check the evidence.

**In practice** (judgment). Successful AFM papers pair a full characterization with a performance comparison against recent literature, usually as a table or a property chart, and a working demonstration. The comparison with "the latest international research" matters: an introduction whose newest reference is four years old invites the "insufficiently original" verdict.

## 3. Carbohydrate Polymers

**Who reads it.** Polysaccharide scientists: chemists, food scientists, biochemists and materials scientists who work on cellulose, starch, chitosan, alginate, pectin and their relatives. The Editors-in-Chief are academics in that field. This is a specialist readership, the opposite of Matter's: the reader knows what a degree of substitution is and will look for its value before reading the results.

**The bar.** Four rules stated at the top of the guide (details in `carbpol-requirements.md` §2):

1. A named carbohydrate polymer is the main focus of the paper and of its title, and its role is the major part of the work.
2. That polymer is well characterized, purchased material included. The guide says editors are unlikely to send a paper for review when the glycan is not adequately characterized.
3. The research is innovative and advances scientific knowledge.
4. The paper states a hypothesis, sets it against the most relevant earlier literature, and says why proving it matters.

The guide also publishes a list of topics it does not want: among them work whose main polymer is lignin, polylactic acid or a polyalkanoate; biocomposites where the polysaccharide is not the major part of the study; chars; routine nanocellulose from another plant source; extraction yields without characterization; a drug with a single cyclodextrin; polysaccharides used only as a support.

**How the first screen works.** An academic editor decides, and the journal page reports six days from submission to first decision. The editor can check three things from the title page and the Materials and methods alone: is the paper in scope, is the polysaccharide described, is the format followed. Unlike the other two journals, Carbohydrate Polymers has no free-format first submission: the word limit (8,000 excluding references), the 200-word abstract, line numbers, the graphical abstract and the position of the Materials and methods all apply on day one.

**What the form of the paper tells you about the audience.**

- The title names the polysaccharide. Readers search by polymer.
- Materials and methods comes directly after the Introduction and may not be moved to the supplement. This readership judges the material before it reads the results.
- A separate five-page guideline lists what must be reported for each polysaccharide: molecular weight with its method and type of average, degree of deacetylation for chitosan, M/G ratio for alginate, dimensions and surface charge for nanocellulose, and so on.
- The hypothesis is how novelty and significance are argued. The journal does not ask for a broad-audience statement; it asks what was expected and why that expectation was worth testing.
- Citations are author and year. The reader recognizes the groups in the field by name.

**In practice** (judgment). Papers that suit this journal explain how a feature of the polysaccharide (molecular weight, degree and pattern of substitution, charge, crystallinity, branching, chain conformation) produces a property, or they report a new modification chemistry or analytical method for polysaccharides. A performance paper in which the polysaccharide could be exchanged for another polymer without changing the story is what the scope rule is written against. A draft written for AFM (function first, polymer as an ingredient) needs more than a new layout to fit here: it needs the polysaccharide's structure to carry the explanation, and the data to show it.

## 4. Choosing between them

When the author has not chosen, or asks which journal suits the paper, decide from the draft and say why in two or three sentences. Do not give odds of acceptance; nobody can.

| The draft's strength | Better fit |
|---|---|
| A new concept, mechanism, phenomenon or design principle that transfers to other materials | Matter |
| A discovery or first measurement on a new material system (MAP 1–2) | Matter |
| A story a materials scientist in another subfield would want to read; societal relevance that can be stated plainly | Matter |
| A material with a clearly measured function, thorough characterization and a performance comparison | AFM |
| A device or application demonstration built on a material advance | AFM (or Matter at MAP 4–5 if the idea behind it is new) |
| A long, data-rich paper with many figures and a detailed Experimental Section | AFM (typical range to 8,000 words, 3–8 display items) |
| A short, sharp report of one surprising result | Matter (considers 2,000–4,000-word reports) |
| A structure–property relationship of a named polysaccharide, shown across molecular weights, degrees of substitution or sources | Carbohydrate Polymers |
| A new chemical, enzymatic or physical modification of a polysaccharide, or an analytical method for polysaccharides | Carbohydrate Polymers |
| A polysaccharide-based material whose advance is its performance, with routine polymer chemistry | AFM; Carbohydrate Polymers only if the polysaccharide's structure explains the performance |
| A paper whose main polymer is lignin, PLA, a polyhydroxyalkanoate or a protein | Matter or AFM; outside the scope of Carbohydrate Polymers |

Practical differences the author may care about:

| | Matter | AFM | Carbohydrate Polymers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Readers | The whole materials community | Materials scientists across fields | Polysaccharide specialists |
| Editors | In-house professionals | In-house professionals | Academic Editors-in-Chief |
| First submission | Any layout | Any layout | The journal's format, with line numbers |
| Abstract | "Summary", 150 words, any voice | 200 words, present tense, no "we" | 200 words |
| Length | No limit; typically 5,000–7,000 words | No limit; typically 3,000–8,000 words | At most 8,000 words excluding references; about 15 figures and tables |
| Extra pieces to write | Progress and potential, MAP level, Highlights, eTOC blurb, graphical abstract | ToC text and graphic, Statistical Analysis subsection | Hypothesis statement, graphical abstract (at submission), polysaccharide characterization data, CRediT statement |
| Structure | Introduction, Results (finding-style subheadings), Discussion, Methods | Introduction, Results and Discussion, Conclusion, Experimental Section | Introduction, Materials and methods, Results and discussion, Conclusions |
| Citations | Superscript numbers | Numbers in square brackets | Author and year (APA) |
| Inquiry before submitting | Welcomed (matter@cell.com) | Not offered in the guide | Not offered for research papers |

If the paper could go to more than one, say so and recommend the one whose bar the paper's strongest element meets. If the author has already chosen, respect the choice, give the fit verdict for that journal, and mention another only when the fit is poor, saying in one clause whether it would suit the paper better or not.

## 5. When the paper fits none of them

Say it plainly and early; it is the most useful thing the author can hear before spending a submission. Typical cases:

- **Incremental.** A blend, filler loading or composition series on a known system with improved numbers and no new understanding or function.
- **Characterization only.** A material is made and described; nothing shows what it is for (AFM) or what was learned that others can use (Matter).
- **The case is environmental.** The paper's main claim is that the route is greener or the feedstock renewable. That is a claim for a sustainability-focused journal; none of these three journals will weigh it as the main advance.
- **Too narrow.** The advance matters to one technique or one polymer family. (For Carbohydrate Polymers a single polymer family is the right width; the question there is whether the work is routine.)
- **Out of scope for Carbohydrate Polymers.** The topic is on the journal's not-of-interest list, or the polysaccharide is a carrier, matrix or support and the paper is about what it carries.
- **The polysaccharide is not described.** A fit problem at Carbohydrate Polymers only, and one the author can often close from a supplier's certificate or a measurement they already have.

What to tell the author: (a) what specific addition would change the verdict, drawn from what the draft hints at ("the humidity-sensing demonstration in Figure S9 is the function; it belongs in the main text with a comparison to published sensors"); (b) that all three publishers run transfer schemes (Wiley's Refer & Transfer program; Cell Press transfers to sister journals; Elsevier's Article Transfer Service, which for Carbohydrate Polymers often means its open-access companion *Carbohydrate Polymer Technologies and Applications*) which the editor may offer; (c) that a more specialized journal may be the faster route. Then deliver the edit in the same reply, as well as it can be done; do not stop to ask whether they still want it. The decision to submit is theirs.

## 6. Fit questions to answer from the draft

Answer these before editing. Each answer must point to a place in the draft; "not in the draft" is an answer, and it goes into the report. When you have only part of a paper, answer what that part allows and record the others as "cannot tell from this part" (questions 7 and 8 usually need the Results; question 11 needs the Materials and methods); give the verdict "on the parts I have seen" and do not count unseen sections as missing evidence.

1. In one sentence, what is the advance? (A result, or a concept supported by results?)
2. What was the best prior result or understanding, with its number and reference? Is it from the last two or three years?
3. What number in this paper beats or explains it, and under what conditions?
4. Who outside the subfield would care, and why? (Matter: the general-interest test. AFM: "beyond specialists in the field". Carbohydrate Polymers: skip this one; ask instead what a polysaccharide scientist learns.)
5. AFM: what is the function, where is it measured, where is it demonstrated in use?
6. Matter: which MAP level, and what is the conceptual advance in a sentence?
7. Is there a mechanism, and is it tested (a control, a spectrum, a model) or only asserted?
8. Are the key numbers replicated, with error?
9. Does the title say this? Does the abstract? Does the last paragraph of the introduction?

For Carbohydrate Polymers, also:

10. Which named carbohydrate polymer is the paper about? Is it in the title? Would the story survive if another polymer replaced it? (If yes, the polysaccharide is not the focus.) Is the topic on the not-of-interest list?
11. For each polysaccharide, purchased ones included: which of the items in `carbpol-requirements.md` §5 does the draft give, and which are missing?
12. What hypothesis did the work test? Is it stated, is it set against the nearest earlier work, and do the conclusions answer it?

Turn the answers into the fit verdict that opens the report:

> **Fit for Matter: reasonable, with one gap.** The paper's idea is that reversible lignin–PVA hydrogen bonds dissipate energy, and Figures 3–4 test it with a methylated-lignin control. That is a transferable concept. The gap: the title, summary and introduction present the work as "a tough hydrogel" and never state the idea. I have rewritten them around it. The comparison with published tough hydrogels (Table S2) should move into the main text.

> **Fit for Carbohydrate Polymers: in scope, but the chitosan is not described yet.** The paper is about chitosan itself: it shows how the degree of deacetylation changes the crystallinity and the water vapor permeability of cast films, which is the kind of structure–property question the journal lists. Two things would stop it at the editor's desk. The chitosans are identified only by supplier and "medium molecular weight"; the journal wants the molecular weight with its method and the type of average. And the hypothesis is implied but never stated. I have stated it in the Introduction from your own aims paragraph; please confirm the wording.

## 7. Reframing without inventing

Fitting the audience means re-ordering and re-stating what the paper already contains. It never means adding claims.

- **Lead with the finding the journal's readers care about.** For Matter, the concept and its evidence; for AFM, the function and its number against the benchmark; for Carbohydrate Polymers, the polysaccharide, the structural feature that was varied or established, and what it does to the property. The synthesis is the means.
- **Move the comparison forward.** If the draft compares with prior work only in the supplement or the last page, bring the key numbers into the introduction and the abstract.
- **Translate subfield shorthand.** Define the abbreviation, name the property in general terms, say in half a sentence why the number matters ("an oxygen permeability below that of PET, the material used for carbonated-drink bottles"). For Carbohydrate Polymers the direction reverses: keep the carbohydrate vocabulary exact, and explain the vocabulary of the application (a device figure of merit, a clinical term) for a reader who is a polysaccharide chemist.
- **Scale the outlook to the evidence.** One sentence on what the result enables, tied to a number in the paper. For Matter, the fuller outlook belongs in Progress and potential.
- **State the hypothesis the paper already implies.** For Carbohydrate Polymers, turn the draft's aim ("we investigated whether ...", "this work explores the effect of ...") into one declarative sentence and ask the author to confirm it. If the draft gives no basis for one, leave a marker; a hypothesis written after the fact by an editor is an invented claim.
- **Leave out what the paper cannot support.** If the draft lacks a function test, a mechanism control or a benchmark, write the frame that the existing data support, put an `[AUTHOR: ...]` query where the missing piece would go, and rank that query first in the to-do list.

When reframing changes emphasis, quote the old and new wording in the change log so the author can veto it.
