# Deliverable templates

Formats for what the skill hands back, for Matter, Advanced Functional Materials (AFM) and Carbohydrate Polymers. The reader is a scientist who is not comfortable with software or with these journals' conventions, so the reply opens with the answer and the actions, and the detail follows. Scale to the input (table in SKILL.md, Deliver): a full manuscript gets every part; part of a paper gets the verdict, the to-do list, the edited part, a list of what the other sections will need and the change log; a pasted abstract gets the edited text with its count, two lines on fit and a few queries.

The Matter and AFM examples below describe a hypothetical paper on a lignin–poly(vinyl alcohol) hydrogel. The Carbohydrate Polymers examples describe a different hypothetical paper, on chitosan films, because a lignin paper is outside that journal's scope. All numbers are placeholders for the format. Never carry them into a real manuscript.

## 1. Order of the reply

1. Verdict (§2)
2. What you still need to do (§3)
3. The edited manuscript (§4, §5 or §6)
4. The journal's extra pieces (§7, §8 or §9) and the cover letter (§12)
5. Change log (§10)
6. Checklist status (§11)

If the chat can create files, put the edited manuscript in a .docx (and Matter's Highlights and eTOC blurb in a second, small .docx, because the journal wants them as a separate file), and keep parts 1, 2, 5 and 6 in the reply itself. If it cannot, give everything in the reply as text that pastes cleanly into Word, with the manuscript between two lines reading `START OF THE EDITED PAPER` and `END OF THE EDITED PAPER` so the author knows what to select. Use bold for headings inside it; a line such as "1. Introduction" must not turn into a numbered list, nor the corresponding-author line that starts with an asterisk into a bullet. If one reply cannot hold it all, send parts 1 and 2 with the title and abstract first, then the manuscript section by section, then parts 4–6, and end each message with one line telling the author what to type to get the next part.

Carbohydrate Polymers wants page numbers and continuous line numbers in the submitted file. If you write the .docx yourself and your tools can switch both on, do it and say so. Otherwise give the Word steps in the to-do list (example in §3).

## 2. Verdict

Three or four sentences, no jargon about the tooling. State the journal, one of three levels, and the reasons. The level follows from what the author still has to do:

- **Ready once the items below are done.** The paper fits the journal and what remains is information only the authors have (names, statements, a form).
- **Close. Fix these first.** The paper fits, and one or two gaps in evidence or framing would probably stop it at the editor's desk; rewriting, or data the authors already hold, can close them.
- **Not ready yet.** Either the paper is the right kind for the journal but needs new measurements or comparisons before it would pass the editor, or it belongs in a different journal. Say which of the two it is.

When the fit is poor, a second short paragraph may say what would change the verdict, whether another of the three journals or a more specialized one would suit better, and that the editor may offer a transfer. With only part of a paper, mark the verdict "on the parts I have seen" and do not treat unseen sections as missing evidence.

Examples:

```
VERDICT (Matter): close. Fix two things first.

Your paper has what Matter asks for: an idea other researchers can reuse (lignin toughens
the gel through reversible hydrogen bonds) and a test of it (the methylated-lignin control
in Figure 4). Two things would likely stop it at the editor's desk: (1) there is no
comparison with published tough hydrogels, so the editor cannot see how large the advance
is; (2) the key toughness values have no replicate numbers or error. I have rewritten the
title, summary and introduction around the idea, put the paper into Matter's layout, and
drafted the pieces Matter asks for (Highlights, the short "In brief" text, the Progress and
potential statement, a MAP level). None of your data were changed, and the change log quotes
every claim I reworded.
```

```
VERDICT (Advanced Functional Materials): not ready yet. It is the right kind of paper, but
the evidence is not there yet.

AFM looks for a material that performs a measured function, and yours does: the hydrogel
works as a strain sensor and you show it on a finger. As written, an editor cannot see that
it is an advance, because the only comparison is with one earlier hydrogel and the main
numbers have no repeat measurements. One point could get the paper returned unread: an
ingredient is described only as "proprietary", and AFM requires every composition to be
disclosed. I have put the paper into AFM's layout and style without changing your data;
items 1 to 3 below are what would change this verdict.
```

```
VERDICT (Carbohydrate Polymers): close. Fix two things first.

Your paper is the kind this journal asks for: it is about chitosan itself, and it shows how
one structural feature, the degree of deacetylation, changes the crystallinity of the film
and its barrier to water vapor. Two things would likely stop it at the editor's desk: (1) the
three chitosans are described only by supplier and "medium molecular weight", and this
journal returns papers whose polysaccharide is not fully described; (2) the paper never
states the hypothesis it tested. I have put the paper into the journal's layout, changed the
citations to author and year, and stated the hypothesis from your own aims paragraph for you
to confirm. None of your data were changed, and the change log quotes every claim I reworded.
```

Do not predict acceptance and do not grade the science beyond what the journal's own criteria ask.

## 3. "What you still need to do"

Numbered, most important first, in three groups. Each item says what to do, where, and why in one line. Write as you would to a colleague who has never submitted to this journal. Merge related items so the list stays readable: about ten for a full paper is a good ceiling, and the detail belongs in the markers.

```
WHAT YOU STILL NEED TO DO

These decide whether the editor sends the paper to reviewers:
1. Add a table comparing your gel with published tough hydrogels (toughness, strain at break,
   water content, reference). Where: I left a marker after the first paragraph of the Results.
   Why: without it the editor cannot judge the size of the advance.
2. Give the number of samples and the ± value for each toughness and strength figure (Results,
   Figure 2 legend). Matter asks for means and standard deviations from replicates.

Needed before you can submit:
3. Fill in the authors' details: full names, affiliations, the corresponding author's email,
   and which one author is the "lead contact" (the single person the journal writes to).
4. Complete the "declaration of interests" form. It is a PDF linked on Matter's Information
   for authors page, in the part called "Submit your manuscript", under "Other files". The
   journal requires it with the first submission.
5. Confirm the AI-use statement I added near the end of the paper (see the note below).
6. Check the Progress and potential statement and the MAP level I proposed (level 3). You know
   better than I do whether they are fair.

Can wait until the journal asks (I have drafted them so they are ready):
7. Highlights and the "In brief" text: read them and correct anything that is not exactly true.
8. Graphical abstract: a single square picture. I have described a layout; it has to be drawn
   by you or a colleague, and not with an AI image generator (the journal does not allow that).
9. Resource availability: choose the sentences that are true for your data (marked in the text).

How to get the paper into Word: open a new, empty Word document. In this chat, select the
text from the line START OF THE EDITED PAPER to the line END OF THE EDITED PAPER, press
Ctrl+C (Cmd+C on a Mac), click in the Word document and press Ctrl+V (Cmd+V on a Mac).

How to find my markers: every place where only you can supply the information is marked
[AUTHOR: ...]. In Word, press Ctrl+F (Cmd+F on a Mac), type [AUTHOR and step through them.
There are 11 in the paper and 3 more in the cover letter. Replace each marker, brackets
included, with the information it asks for.

Formatting that plain text cannot carry: make the "g" in Tg a subscript and put the symbols
T and E in italics.

About the AI-use statement: the journal asks authors to say when an AI assistant helped
prepare the text. Because I edited this manuscript, I added that statement. Please keep it,
fill in the details, and read the whole paper before submitting: you are responsible for
every sentence in it.
```

Rules for this list: count the markers and give the true number (the script reports it); never list something you already did; keep each item to an action the author can take; put anything that needs a new experiment or measurement in the first group and say plainly that it is their decision whether to add it or to submit without it. Missing replicates and error values on key numbers belong in the first group for all three journals. When the edited paper went into a .docx, drop the copying paragraph. When figures or captions were not supplied, add one item asking the author to place each figure and its caption after the paragraph that first cites it.

**Carbohydrate Polymers.** The first group holds the scope, characterization and hypothesis items. The third group is almost empty, because this journal wants its format, the graphical abstract and the declarations with the first submission; say so in one line. The copying, marker and AI paragraphs are the same as above.

```

## 7. Matter's extra pieces

Count every one of these before delivering (script, or by hand character by character). Show the count next to each so the author can see it fits.

**Highlights** (up to four; each at most 85 characters with spaces; no abbreviations):

```
HIGHLIGHTS
• Lignin doubles the toughness of a poly(vinyl alcohol) hydrogel            (62 characters)
• Blocking lignin's hydroxyl groups removes the toughening effect           (63)
• Reversible hydrogen bonds dissipate energy and re-form within 30 minutes  (72)
• The same approach toughens gels made with two other plant polyphenols     (69)
```

Each is a finding, stated as a sentence with a verb. Weak: "Novel lignin hydrogel was successfully prepared" (says nothing, uses a discouraged word). Spell out polymer names; "PVA" is an abbreviation.

**eTOC blurb / In brief** (at most 50 words, two sentences, third person, for scientists outside the field):

```
IN BRIEF
Tough hydrogels usually rely on synthetic crosslinkers. Doe et al. show that lignin, a
by-product of papermaking, toughens a poly(vinyl alcohol) hydrogel through reversible
hydrogen bonds that break under load and re-form at rest, a mechanism that extends to other
plant polyphenols.                                                              (42 words)
```

First sentence: the context. Second: who showed what, and why it matters. Open the second sentence with the first author's surname and "et al."; if no author names were supplied, write `[AUTHOR: first author's surname] et al.` and count the marker as one word.

**Progress and potential** (one or two paragraphs for non-experts; within 1,000 characters with spaces to satisfy both of the journal's stated limits; at most 300 words in any case):

```
PROGRESS AND POTENTIAL
Soft, water-rich materials called hydrogels are used in wound dressings, soft robots and
wearable sensors, but most tear easily. Making them tough usually requires synthetic
additives and several processing steps. This work shows that lignin, a plant polymer that
paper mills burn as low-value fuel, can do the job by forming weak bonds that break under
stress and re-form afterwards. The gel absorbs twice as much energy before breaking as the
same gel without lignin and recovers most of its toughness within 30 minutes. Because the
effect comes from a general chemical interaction, other plant-derived molecules can be used
in the same way. In the longer term, this could give manufacturers a simple route to durable
soft materials made largely from renewable by-products. The gels have so far been tested
only at laboratory scale and at room temperature.                       (858 characters)
```

It answers the journal's three questions (why important, longer-term ambition, impact on society) in that order, uses no abbreviations, and ends on an honest limit. Outlook is allowed here and must follow from the paper's results.

**MAP proposal** (one level, one or two sentences of justification, optional pointer to earlier key papers):

```
PROPOSED MAP LEVEL: 3 – Understanding
The study extends known lignin–poly(vinyl alcohol) hydrogels by identifying and testing the
toughening mechanism and mapping how toughness depends on lignin content. It does not
demonstrate a device or application, so level 4 would overstate it.
[AUTHOR: confirm. Earlier key work leading to this study can be cited here, e.g. refs 12 and 18.]
```

**Graphical abstract** (a described layout only; never an image):

```

Square, single panel, reading left to right.
Left: a wood chip and the words "lignin (papermaking by-product)".
Center: a sketch of the gel network with dashed lines for hydrogen bonds; one arrow "stretch →

Right: a stretched strip of gel with the one key number, "2× tougher".

[AUTHOR: check the exact size and file type in the Cell Press graphical abstract guide.]
```

## 8. AFM's extra pieces

```

## 9. Carbohydrate Polymers' extra pieces

The journal has no Highlights, blurb or impact statement in its guide. What it asks for instead is scientific: a stated hypothesis and a described polysaccharide. The first is drafted from the author's own aims; the second can only be asked for.

**Hypothesis statement** (in the Introduction, usually its last paragraph; three parts: the hypothesis, what is distinct about it, why it matters):

```
HYPOTHESIS (last paragraph of the Introduction)
We hypothesized that raising the degree of deacetylation of chitosan from 75% to 95%, at
constant molecular weight, lowers the water vapor permeability of cast films, because more
regular chains pack into a more crystalline film. Earlier comparisons used chitosans that
differed in both degree of deacetylation and molecular weight (Author et al., Year), so the
two effects could not be separated. If the hypothesis holds, the barrier of a chitosan film
can be set by the choice of chitosan, without additives.
[AUTHOR: please confirm that this is the hypothesis you set out to test; I took it from your
aims paragraph]
```

Declarative and testable: it names the polysaccharide, the variable, the expected direction and the reason. The second and third sentences (what is distinct, why it matters) also come from the draft: its own account of the earlier work and its stated motivation. Where the draft offers nothing for one of them, put a short marker in its place; do not write the authors' significance for them. Weak: "This study aims to investigate the properties of chitosan films" (an aim, with nothing to confirm or refute). Take it from what the draft says the authors expected or set out to find. If the draft gives no basis, do not compose one: leave `[AUTHOR: state in one sentence what you expected to find and why; the journal asks for a hypothesis]` and put it in the first group of the to-do list. The Conclusions then say whether it was confirmed.

## 14. Plain-language glossary

Use these explanations the first time a term appears in the reply. One line each is enough.

| Term | Say |
|---|---|
| Summary (Matter) | Matter's name for the abstract. |
| Highlights | Up to four one-line findings shown at the top of the published article. |
| eTOC blurb / In brief | A two-sentence description used on the article's first page and in the journal's email alerts. |
| Progress and potential | A short box, written for non-specialists, on why the work matters and where it could lead. |
| MAP level | Matter's 1-to-6 label for how far a material is from practical use; you propose it, the editor confirms it. |
| Lead contact | The one author the journal and readers write to. |
| Graphical abstract | One picture that shows the main message; made by the authors. Square for Matter; wide for Carbohydrate Polymers, which wants it with the first submission. |
| Resource availability | A required section saying how others can obtain your materials, data and code. |
| Declaration of interests | A statement, plus a form, about financial or other interests related to the work. |
| Supplemental information (Matter) / Supporting Information (AFM) / Supplementary material (Carbohydrate Polymers) | The extra file with additional figures and data, published online with the paper. At Carbohydrate Polymers the methods may not be put there. |
| ToC entry (AFM) | A 50–60-word text and a small picture shown in the journal's table of contents. |
| Data Availability Statement | One sentence saying where the data behind the paper can be found. |
| Statistical Analysis subsection | A short paragraph at the end of the Experimental Section on sample numbers and tests. |
| ORCID iD | A free personal researcher number from orcid.org; the journal asks for it when you submit. |
| Editorial Manager / Wiley Authors | The websites where the files are uploaded (Matter and Carbohydrate Polymers / AFM). |
| Free-format submission | The first version may be in any layout; the journal's layout is required only when you revise. Matter and AFM offer this; Carbohydrate Polymers does not. |
| Presubmission inquiry (Matter) | An email to the editors with the title and abstract asking whether the paper suits the journal. |
| Desk rejection | A decision by the editor, without reviewers, that the paper does not fit the journal. |
| Point-by-point response | The letter sent with a revised paper answering each reviewer comment in turn. |
| CRediT | A standard list of roles (conceptualization, investigation, writing ...) used to describe who did what. Carbohydrate Polymers requires a statement in this form. |
| Marker | A note from me inside your text, written [AUTHOR: ...], at a place where only you have the information. |
| Cover letter | A half-page letter to the editor, uploaded with the paper, saying what the paper shows and why it suits the journal. |
| Title page (AFM) | The first page of the manuscript file: title, all authors with affiliations and emails, and the required statements. |
| Title Case | Capital first letters on the main words of a title. |
| DOI | The permanent identifier of an article or data set, starting with "10."; it goes at the end of each reference. |
| Transfer | An offer from the editor to pass a declined paper to a sister journal of the same publisher. |
| Hypothesis statement (Carbohydrate Polymers) | One sentence in the Introduction saying what you expected to find and set out to test. |
| Characterization of the polysaccharide (Carbohydrate Polymers) | The basic data on the polysaccharide you used (purity, molecular weight, degree of substitution or deacetylation ...), given in the Materials section even for a bought product. |
| Certificate of analysis | The supplier's sheet of measured values for the batch you bought; ask the supplier for it by batch number. |
| Declarations tool (Carbohydrate Polymers) | An Elsevier web form about competing interests (declarations.elsevier.com); it gives you a Word file to upload with the paper. |
| Line numbers | Numbers in the margin of every line so that reviewers can point to a place. In Word: Layout tab, Line Numbers, Continuous. |
| Author–year citations (APA style) | Earlier work is cited as (Surname, 2021) in the text, and the reference list is in alphabetical order without numbers. |
| Data repository | A public website that stores research data and gives it a permanent link (a university repository, Mendeley Data, Zenodo). |
| Dispersity (Đ) | The current name for what used to be called the polydispersity index (PDI) of a polymer's molecular weight. |
