Techno-Economic and Life Cycle Assessment of an Integrated Corn Stover Biorefinery Co-Producing Cellulosic Ethanol and Lignin-Derived Polyurethane Foam

Abstract

We evaluate an integrated biorefinery that converts corn stover into cellulosic ethanol and a lignin-derived polyol for rigid polyurethane foam. Process models were built in Aspen Plus for a 2000 dry ton/day facility and used to estimate a minimum ethanol selling price (MESP) and cradle-to-gate greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Co-producing the lignin polyol reduces the MESP from $2.85/gal to $2.21/gal and reduces GHG emissions relative to a base case where lignin is combusted for heat and power. Sensitivity analysis shows that polyol selling price and lignin extraction yield are the dominant economic drivers. The results demonstrate that lignin valorization is a sustainable and economically attractive route to improve biorefinery viability.

Introduction

Cellulosic ethanol production from agricultural residues has been extensively studied, and detailed techno-economic analyses (TEAs) by NREL have established benchmark process designs and costs (Humbird et al., 2011). In these designs, lignin is burned in a boiler to supply process heat and export electricity. Because lignin represents 15-25% of stover mass and carries a large fraction of its energy content, this is a reasonable but low-value use. Numerous authors have argued that lignin valorization to chemicals or materials could improve the economics of biorefineries substantially.

Polyols for polyurethane foams are one candidate product. Lignin contains aliphatic and phenolic hydroxyl groups that can react with isocyanates, and oxypropylated lignin has been shown to give rigid foams with properties comparable to petroleum-based foams. However, few studies have quantified the process-level economic and environmental consequences of diverting lignin from the boiler to a polyol product, which requires replacing the lost process heat with natural gas or additional biomass.

Here we develop process models for two configurations of a corn stover biorefinery: (i) a base case following the NREL 2011 design, in which lignin is combusted, and (ii) a co-production case in which lignin is extracted after enzymatic hydrolysis, oxypropylated, and sold as a polyol. We compare the cases on MESP and cradle-to-gate GHG emissions and identify the parameters that most affect the comparison.

Methods

Process Design

Both cases process 2000 dry metric tons per day of corn stover with dilute-acid pretreatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, and co-fermentation of glucose and xylose by Zymomonas mobilis. Downstream distillation and molecular sieve dehydration follow the NREL design. In the co-production case, the hydrolysis residue is washed, and lignin is dissolved in aqueous NaOH at 80 °C, filtered, precipitated with H2SO4, and dried. Oxypropylation is carried out with propylene oxide at 150 °C and 10 bar with KOH catalyst. Details of the unit operations are given in the Supporting Information.

Techno-Economic Analysis

Capital costs were estimated from Aspen Process Economic Analyzer and scaled from literature values using the six-tenths rule. Operating costs include feedstock at $84/dry ton, enzymes, chemicals, labor, and maintenance. A discounted cash flow analysis with a 10% internal rate of return and a 30 year plant life was used to compute the MESP. All costs are in 2022 dollars.

Life Cycle Assessment

Cradle-to-gate GHG emissions were computed with process inventories from the Aspen models and background data from Ecoinvent 3.8. Impacts were characterized using the IPCC 2013 GWP100 method. Feedstock production emissions were taken from the GREET model. Electricity exported to the grid was credited using the US average grid mix.

Results and Discussion

Economic Performance

Table 1 summarizes capital and operating costs. The co-production case requires an additional $58 million of capital for lignin extraction and oxypropylation, an increase of 13% over the base case. However, polyol revenue of $1.80/kg at a yield of 0.38 kg polyol per kg of lignin in stover more than offsets the additional capital and the cost of natural gas needed to replace lignin in the boiler. The MESP falls from $2.85/gal in the base case to $2.21/gal in the co-production case.

Figure 2 shows the sensitivity of the MESP to key parameters. Polyol selling price has the largest effect: at $1.20/kg the MESP advantage disappears, and at $2.40/kg the MESP falls to $1.74/gal. Lignin extraction yield is the second most important parameter. Enzyme cost and feedstock cost have similar effects in both cases and therefore do not change the comparison.

Environmental Performance

Cradle-to-gate GHG emissions are shown in Figure 3. In the base case, the biorefinery exports electricity, which provides a credit. In the co-production case, the electricity credit is lost and natural gas combustion adds emissions, but the polyol displaces petroleum-based polyol. The net effect is a reduction in GHG emissions of 18% for the co-production case. The result is sensitive to the assumed emissions of the displaced petroleum polyol and to the grid mix used for the electricity credit. With a low-carbon grid mix, the base case credit shrinks and the co-production advantage grows.

We note that the oxypropylation step uses propylene oxide, which is petroleum-derived and contributes 34% of the co-production case GHG emissions. Bio-based propylene oxide or alternative modification chemistries would further improve the environmental profile.

Comparison with Prior Work

Our base case MESP of $2.85/gal is consistent with previous updates of the NREL design. Prior estimates of the economic benefit of lignin valorization vary widely depending on the assumed product and price. Our result falls within the range reported for lignin-to-adhesive and lignin-to-carbon-fiber routes.

Conclusions

Co-producing a lignin-derived polyol in a corn stover ethanol biorefinery reduces the MESP by 22% and cradle-to-gate GHG emissions by 18% relative to combusting lignin, under the assumptions used here. Polyol price and lignin extraction yield dominate the economics, and the emissions of displaced petroleum polyol and the electricity credit dominate the environmental result. Lignin valorization is a sustainable and green route to improved biorefinery viability.
